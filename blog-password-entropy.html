TRANSMISSION_005 DEBUNKED

Your Password Policy Is Mathematically Wrong

2026-09-10·~11 MIN READ·CLEARANCE: PUBLIC
EntropyPasswordsSecurity HashcatInformation Theory

Every corporate IT policy in the world will tell you the same thing: your password needs at least 8 characters, one uppercase letter, one lowercase letter, one number, and one special character. This advice is not just annoying — it is mathematically wrong. A password made of 4 random English words is harder to crack, easier to remember, and has more entropy. Here's the proof.

00 // THE TWO PASSWORDS

Let's compare two real passwords:

[FAIL] "COMPLEX" PASSWORD (TRADITIONAL POLICY)
Tr0ub4dor&3
11 chars · Upper, lower, digit, special · ~28 bits of entropy
[PASS] PASSPHRASE (4 RANDOM DICTIONARY WORDS)
correct horse battery staple
28 chars · Lowercase only · ~44 bits of entropy (conservative)

The first password looks secure. It has a capital letter, numbers replacing vowels, an ampersand, and a trailing digit. It checks every box on every password policy in existence. But it has roughly 28 bits of entropy.

The second password is four common English words. No capitals, no numbers, no special characters. It fails most password policies. But it has roughly 44 bits of entropy — meaning it's 216 = 65,536 times harder to crack.

How? Entropy doesn't care about how a password looks. It cares about how it was generated.

01 // SHANNON ENTROPY — THE MATH

Claude Shannon defined information entropy in 1948. For passwords, the relevant measure is:

$$H = \log_2(N^L) = L \cdot \log_2(N)$$

where \(N\) is the size of the symbol pool and \(L\) is the length of the password in symbols. The entropy \(H\) (in bits) tells you how many binary guesses an attacker needs to exhaust the entire search space.

Character-Level Analysis

If you pick characters uniformly at random from a pool: 

POOL                         SIZE (N)    BITS PER CHAR
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Lowercase only               26          4.70
Lowercase + uppercase         52          5.70
+ digits                     62          5.95
+ special chars (~32)        94          6.55

A truly random 11-character password from the full 94-char pool gives:

$$H = 11 \times \log_2(94) \approx 11 \times 6.55 = 72.1 \text{ bits}$$

72 bits! That's excellent. But here's the problem: nobody generates passwords this way.

02 // THE HUMAN FACTOR — WHERE ENTROPY DIES

Real humans don't sample uniformly from character pools. They do this:

  1. Pick a dictionary word they can remember (e.g., "troubador")
  2. Capitalize the first letter (always the first — never the fourth)
  3. Replace vowels with obvious substitutions: a→4, o→0, e→3, i→1
  4. Append a special character (usually &, !, or @)
  5. Append a single digit (usually 1, 2, or 3)

An attacker who knows this pattern — and they all do — doesn't need to search 9411 possibilities. They need to search:

$$\underbrace{\sim 50{,}000}_{\text{common words}} \times \underbrace{2^4}_{\text{leet substitutions}} \times \underbrace{\sim 10}_{\text{special chars}} \times \underbrace{10}_{\text{trailing digit}} \approx 8 \times 10^7$$

That's \(\log_2(8 \times 10^7) \approx 26.3\) bits. Not 72. Twenty-six bits. Hashcat on a single RTX 4090 cracks this in under a second.

"The entropy of a password is determined by the process that generated it — not by the character set it happens to contain."

03 // DICEWARE — THE RIGHT WAY

The Diceware method (invented by Arnold Reinhold, 1995) generates passphrases by rolling dice to select words from a list:

  1. Take a word list of \(N\) words (the standard Diceware list has 7,776 words; the EFF list also has 7,776)
  2. Roll dice (or use a CSPRNG) to pick words uniformly at random
  3. Concatenate the words with spaces

The entropy per word is:

$$H_{\text{word}} = \log_2(7{,}776) \approx 12.9 \text{ bits}$$

For a 4-word passphrase:

$$H = 4 \times 12.9 = 51.7 \text{ bits}$$

Even if you use a more conservative dictionary of ~2,048 common words (the BIP-39 word list used for crypto seed phrases), each word gives \(\log_2(2048) = 11\) bits, so 4 words = 44 bits.

Critical Point: The Attacker Knows Your Method

Kerckhoffs' principle applies here: assume the attacker knows you used Diceware. They know the word list. They know you picked 4 words. Their search space is \(7{,}776^4 = 3.66 \times 10^{15}\) combinations. That's still 51.7 bits of entropy — because the entropy comes from the randomness of selection, not from secrecy of the method.

Compare: Tr0ub4dor&3 has 26 bits even when the attacker doesn't know your exact method, because the generation process is so predictable.

The "complex" password with every special character trick in the book cracks in 7 milliseconds. Four random dictionary words take 29 minutes to 4 days. Five words push you into decades. Six words and you're done — nobody's cracking that before the sun explodes.

05 // BUT WHAT ABOUT DICTIONARY ATTACKS?

Common objection: "If you use dictionary words, attackers just run a dictionary attack!"

This confuses two completely different things:

  • A single dictionary word as a password — yes, this is instant to crack (12.9 bits). Nobody recommends this.
  • Multiple random dictionary words — the entropy multiplies with each word. The attacker must search combinations, not individual words.

A 4-word Diceware passphrase is not a "dictionary attack" target — it's a combinatorial explosion. The attacker isn't matching against a word list; they're brute-forcing through \(7{,}776^4\) ordered permutations. That's fundamentally different from checking "is the password 'horse'?"

The Math of Combination vs. Lookup

$$\text{1 word: } 7{,}776^1 = 7{,}776 \quad (\text{trivial})$$ $$\text{2 words: } 7{,}776^2 = 60.5\text{M} \quad (\text{seconds})$$ $$\text{3 words: } 7{,}776^3 = 470\text{B} \quad (\text{minutes})$$ $$\text{4 words: } 7{,}776^4 = 3.66\text{T} \quad (\text{days})$$ $$\text{5 words: } 7{,}776^5 = 28.4\text{P} \quad (\text{decades})$$

Each additional word multiplies the search space by 7,776×. That's 12.9 bits per word. Linear growth in memorization effort, exponential growth in attack cost. This is the correct tradeoff.

06 // WHY PASSWORD POLICIES ARE BACKWARDS

The NIST Special Publication 800-63B (2017 revision) explicitly recommends against composition rules. Direct quote:

"Verifiers SHOULD NOT impose other composition rules (e.g., requiring mixtures of different character types or prohibiting consecutively repeated characters) for memorized secrets."

— NIST SP 800-63B, Section 5.1.1.2

NIST's reasoning:

  • Composition rules reduce usability without meaningfully increasing entropy (because users respond with predictable patterns)
  • Length is more important than complexity — allow up to 64+ characters
  • Check against breached password lists instead of enforcing character rules
  • Don't force periodic rotation — it causes users to pick weaker passwords

Yet in 2026, virtually every enterprise login page still demands P@ssw0rd1! and rejects correct horse battery staple. The policy isn't protecting users — it's actively making them less secure by forcing them into a predictable generation process that attackers have already optimized for.

07 // THE ENTROPY PER COGNITIVE LOAD METRIC

Let's define a useful metric: entropy per unit of memorization effort.

$$\eta = \frac{H_{\text{bits}}}{\text{cognitive load}}$$

Cognitive load is subjective, but we can approximate it by the number of "chunks" a human must remember (Miller's Law: 7±2 chunks in working memory): 

PASSWORD                 ENTROPY    CHUNKS    η (bits/chunk)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Tr0ub4dor&3              26 bits    ~6*       4.3
J4fS<2                   20 bits    ~6        3.3
correct horse battery    44 bits    4         11.0
staple                   
Random 12-char (94)      78 bits    ~12       6.5

* "Tr0ub4dor&3" has ~6 cognitive chunks: base word + capitalization rule + leet map + special char + digit + position memory.

The passphrase wins by a landslide: 11 bits per cognitive chunk vs 4.3 for the "complex" password. You get 2.5× more security per unit of mental effort. It's not even close.

08 // PRACTICAL RECOMMENDATIONS

Based on the entropy analysis:

  1. Use 5-6 random words from a large word list (EFF Diceware, 7,776 words). This gives 64-77 bits of entropy — sufficient against any current attack.
  2. Generate words using a CSPRNG, not from your head. Human "random" word selection is heavily biased toward common, short words.
  3. Use a password manager for everything except your master password. The master password should be a strong Diceware passphrase.
  4. Enable 2FA. Even a perfect password doesn't protect against phishing or credential stuffing. TOTP or hardware keys (FIDO2) add a second factor that doesn't depend on entropy at all.
  5. Never reuse passwords. The highest-entropy password in the world is useless if it appears in a breach dump. Credential stuffing doesn't care about entropy.

The Actual Threat Model

Most password compromises in 2026 don't come from brute force. They come from:

  • Credential stuffing — reused passwords from breached databases
  • Phishing — user hands over the password voluntarily
  • Session hijacking — stealing tokens, not passwords
  • Social engineering — "what was your first pet's name?"

Against these attacks, password complexity is irrelevant. What matters is uniqueness (different password per site), 2FA, and breach monitoring. The entropy argument only applies to offline brute force — and even there, the passphrase wins.

09 // THE TAKEAWAY

The entire password complexity orthodoxy is based on a flawed premise: that security comes from the character set rather than the generation process. Shannon's entropy formula doesn't care about your exclamation marks. It cares about how many possible passwords your method could have produced.

Four random dictionary words. No capitals. No numbers. No special characters. More entropy than your "complex" password, easier to type, and trivial to remember. The math doesn't lie.

"Through 20 years of effort, we've successfully trained everyone to use passwords that are hard for humans to remember but easy for computers to guess."

— Randall Munroe, xkcd #936
